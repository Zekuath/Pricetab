## [Unreleased]

### Added
- **Read the chart from the keyboard** - with the chart focused (Tab to it), ← and → move a cursor point by point — candle by candle in candle mode — with the crosshair and the axes' labels following, and a screen reader hears each point's time and price. Home and End go to the first and latest point, a step past the edge of a zoomed chart moves the window and reads on, and Esc puts the cursor away. The chart also describes itself to a screen reader in one sentence: the coin, the stretch shown, where it started and ended, and its highest and lowest points
- **Blue and orange for up and down** - a choice under Settings → Basics for red–green colour blindness, the most common kind: every rise and fall in the app, and in the toolbar popup, turns blue or orange instead of green or red, and the comparison's line becomes purple so blue keeps a single meaning
- **How ordinary a call is** - pointing at a square on the calls board now also says how often the price moved that far in that long on the same chart, and + and − zoom the board's reach as they zoom the chart
- **Counted studies on the chart** - six switches in the chart's drawer (V), all off until you turn them on: volume by price, where the price sits in each range, unusual volume, regimes (the days whose last 20 days rose or fell 5%), the usual range (how far 80% of this chart's past stretches travelled, drawn as a cone ahead of the price), and levels where price has turned — each with how often it turned there and how often it turned at any price. Each says what it counts and nothing about what comes next; the notes say what was found when each was tested on four coins' history
- **One coin priced in another** - while comparing two coins, "BTC in ETH" draws the first priced in the second, on an axis of its own
- **Draw on the chart** - a + at the right end of the range buttons, off the chart itself so it never covers what the chart prints, grows (on a press, or after resting the pointer on it for a second) into tools — they slide out of it one by one and the + turns into an × — that draw horizontal lines, trend lines, rays, boxes and notes. They stay with the coin at their price and time on every range and after a reload, move when you drag them, go with Delete, and are listed in the chart's drawer (V). A horizontal line's menu sets a price target at its level. Pointing at a tool says what it does, and "Chart Tools" in the chart's settings hides the + altogether
- **A ruler** - hold Shift and drag across the chart (or pick the ruler) to see the change in price and percent, the time and the number of bars between two points, and how often the price moved that far in that long on the same chart
- **Zoom and pan the chart in time** - scroll (or pinch a trackpad) over the chart to zoom in around the pointer, drag it sideways to move back and forth, press + and − to zoom, and with the chart focused hold Shift and use ← and → to step through time. A chip over the chart says which stretch you are looking at, and "Whole range" or a double-click on the chart puts the range back. The range buttons stay as they were
- **More detail as you zoom** - zoom into a few hours and the chart fetches finer candles (down to one minute) for that stretch, for coins and currencies Coinbase quotes; until they arrive, and wherever they do not reach, it keeps drawing the range's own prices. The 1H range already has finer prices than any candle and fetches nothing
- **A price scale and a time axis on every chart** - round prices down the right-hand side and dates or times along the foot, on the line, the candles, the grid, the board, the log scale and the comparison (which reads in percent). The grid's and the board's own labels moved onto the axes, so a gridline and its price are always the same number. The last price is a tag on the scale with a dotted line across the chart, and pointing at the chart puts the price and the time of the point you are on in pills on the two axes. Saved images include them
- **Old prices say how old they are** - when the price service cannot be reached or you are offline, the chart shows "Prices from 12 min ago" instead of leaving you to guess
- **CoinDesk and The Block in the news** - the two largest crypto newsrooms join the six already behind the optional newsroom permission; like them, nothing is asked of either site until you allow it, one site at a time, in Settings → Permissions or from the news panel
- **Bybit's notices about coins, if you want them** - a "Bybit notices" source in the news panel, off until you press it: the exchange's delisting and network-upgrade notices, each as its own row. Its listings, reward campaigns and giveaways never reach the panel, and nothing is asked of it while it is off
- **The news list is split by day, and can be read by coverage** - a heading for today, yesterday and each earlier day; and a "Most covered" order that puts the stories the most newsrooms ran in the last three days first. Headlines you have already opened are dimmed, a Hacker News row shows how many points the discussion has, and the line about an unusual move now says how rare it was on the chart you are looking at ("6 of the 358 steps moved this far or further")
- **The chart companion explains a mark when you point at it** - hover a numbered ring, a pattern's name, its points or its neckline, or a line of the list in the corner, and the corner shows what that signal is, what it is said to mean, where the price has gone since, and how often it played out on this coin compared with any stretch of the same length, with the size of that count; a pattern also shows its levels and how far today's price is from each. Click to keep it open, click the chart to put it away. The other marks step back while one is being read. It still never calls a signal better or worse
- **Crypto tax guide for every country** - a Tax guide button on the portfolio opens the crypto tax rules of every country and territory — 244 of them. Each card says what is taxed and what is not (buying, holding, moving between your own wallets, selling, swapping, paying with crypto, staking, mining, airdrops), the tax year, the rates and reliefs, what is worth knowing, and the official documents each rule was read in — the tax authority, the law, parliament, a ministry or a central bank, never a news site or a guide — with the day they were read. A country whose rule no official source confirmed says so and states nothing about it. Where a rule is specific enough, your recorded sales and income are matched the way that country does it, with an estimated tax at the rates you enter and a file to download (Form 8949 columns in the US). "What is what" explains the terms and counts what the world does with each event. It is an estimate from what you recorded here, not tax advice
- **A holding in detail** - Details in a holding's editor, or the coin on any record, opens everything about it: value, average cost, unrealized and realized gains, income received, how much has been held over a year, each purchase with its days held, and each sale with what it realized
- **Performance** - under the portfolio chart, the return of what you actually held with the money you put in or took out left out of it, month by month on the 1Y and ALL ranges, with the best and worst month and how many were up
- **Activity** - every purchase, receipt, transfer in and sale in date order, newest first
- **Income records and dates** - the purchase form has a Received mode for staking, rewards and airdrops (recorded at their value when they arrived), and a date field, so a purchase, a receipt or a sale can be recorded on the day it happened
- **Scaled orders** - spread one size across a price range as 3, 5 or 10 waiting orders, placed together or not at all, with your stop and take-profit on each. The ticket lists every level and the size at it before you press
- **Order history** - the Orders tab shows how each of your recent orders ended: filled, cancelled or could not open
- **Comparison says more** - while two coins are compared, the strip counts how often they moved the same way, and the cursor shows the gap between them at that moment
- **The derivatives market is laid out like a trading terminal** - the chart, the order book and the order ticket side by side with your positions under the chart and the book, using the whole window. Drag the seams between them to make the book, the ticket or the positions panel bigger or smaller (or use the arrow keys on a seam); your layout is remembered, and a double-click on a seam puts it back
- **Candlestick chart with volume on the derivatives market** - the contract's own candles with volume bars beneath, the open, high, low and close of any bar under the cursor, and a switch back to a line. The chart says which candles it is drawing
- **Take-profit and stop-loss on limit and stop orders** - the levels you type with a resting order now wait with it, show on the chart and are set on the contract the moment it fills
- **Trade from the order book and the chart** - click a price in the book or on the chart and the ticket is set to a limit order there, on the side that rests at that price. Nothing is placed until you press the order button. The book shows the last price between the two sides and fills the height of its column
- **Settings on the derivatives market itself** - the chart style, the layout, the order confirmation, the contract strip on the main chart and the alarm are in a Settings panel on the page
- **Cancel all orders** - one button, pressed twice, takes back every waiting order and returns its margin
- **Resize the widgets drawer, and size the cards from it** - drag the drawer's right edge to make it wider or narrower (it keeps the window's height); the width is remembered, a double-click on the edge puts the default back, and with the edge focused the arrow keys, Home and End move it. The S / M / L / XL card size is now in the drawer's head beside Choose, and larger cards take fewer, wider columns instead of squeezing into the same ones
- **Two new portfolio charts** - "As held" draws what you actually held on each day, from the dates of your purchases and sales, against a dashed step of what it had cost you by then, shaded green above and red below; "Mix" shows each coin's share of the total through the range, stacked to 100%, so you can see how prices moved your allocation
- **A Regimes widget** - a nine-cell table for the coin on screen: each time it began rising, flat or falling over 20 days, and what it was 20 days later, beside the share of any day. On BTC, ETH, SOL and LTC every cell is within 8 points of an ordinary day, and the card says so; it forecasts nothing
- **Contract warnings leave by themselves** - "close to liquidation", "liquidated" and the stop and take-profit notices fade out after ten seconds on screen, with a thin bar showing the time left; resting the pointer on one holds it
- **Save the chart as an image** - press **I**, or the button at the foot of the chart's drawer (V), and the chart on screen is saved as a picture with the coin, its price, the range and the time above it. Whatever is drawn on it — indicator lines, the companion, the CPI marks — is in the picture. It is made in your browser; nothing is uploaded
- **Save headlines to read later** - the bookmark at the end of a headline keeps it, and "Saved" in the news panel lists what you kept, even after it has left the feed
- **Every panel says its key** - each tab on both side columns shows its key under its name, every drawer and screen shows it beside its title, Settings' menu shows the number that jumps to each section, and the chart settings a key also switches (candles, grid, log scale) show it beside their names
- **Choose which patterns and setups the companion names** - with the chart companion on, a chip for each of the four patterns and twelve strategy setups sits under its switch, both in Settings and in the chart's drawer (V). Press one off and it leaves the chart and the corner list; the others are numbered again from one
- **The chart companion also names what common strategies wait for** - golden and death crosses, MACD crosses, 20-day breakouts and breakdowns, closes outside the Bollinger bands, Supertrend flips, squeezes and ADX rising through 25. When one has happened recently it is numbered on the chart, and a small list in the corner says what it is, when, and how often the price was up ten days later on this coin next to an ordinary day. The base-rate screen (B) lists all twelve. They were tested together on four coins before this was built and none was distinguishable from an ordinary day, so none is called better or worse
- **Indicator lines** - classic indicators drawn from the coin's daily candles, one or several at once: Bollinger bands, the Donchian channel, the 50- and 200-day averages and Supertrend. None clears them all. Settings → The chart → Indicator Lines, or the chart's drawer (V). They are labelled as daily and are not drawn on a range too short to hold five days
- **A chart companion that names the patterns on the chart** - switch on Settings → The chart → Chart Companion and the chart marks the head-and-shoulders, inverse head-and-shoulders, double tops and double bottoms it finds on the coin's daily candles, where they are: the points it read them from, the neckline, the target the pattern's height measures and the level that would undo it. Beside each is the coin's own record — how many times it happened and how often it reached its target first. The base-rate screen (B) lists all four. They were counted before this was built and are rare — sixty in eleven years across four coins — so there is no score and no comparison, only the record
- **US inflation releases are marked on the chart** - each US CPI release shows as a faint line tagged CPI where it falls, and from two days before the next one a line under the price says when it is. The base-rate screen (B) counts what the last twelve did to the coin on screen: how often the half hour after the release moved more than any of the seven half hours before it, against the one-in-eight you would expect if it were an ordinary half hour. The calendar ships with the extension, so it costs no request; switch it off under Settings → The chart → US Releases
- **The order book has a depth curve** - a Depth tab beside Book and Trades on the derivatives page draws how much is resting on each side within a given distance of the middle price, bids and asks on one scale, with a table at 1, 2, 5 and 10 basis points. The curve stops at the last level the book was read to and says how far that was, instead of guessing past it
- **The keyboard shortcuts are a page of Settings now** - press **?** anywhere and Settings opens on them, grouped by where each key works; press **?** again to close. It used to be a separate card over the chart
- **The side columns open when you come near their pulls** - resting the pointer a little way in from either pull, not only on it, slides the pull out and brings its column after the same short wait. Passing by still opens nothing, and a click near the pull still lands on whatever is there
- **Keys for the two side columns and for Settings' menu** - **,** pulls out the chart's column on the left and **.** the screens' column on the right, with the keyboard focus in them so Tab and Enter work from there; with Settings open, **1**–**9** and **0** jump down its menu. Every tab in both columns now says its key when you rest the pointer on it
- **The marks on the chart now say which way the price went** - the "what happened here?" marks were all the same grey triangle whatever had happened. A rise is a green mark pointing up and a fall is a red one pointing down, so you can see what a moment was before you open it
- **Panels now have tabs, so you can move between them without closing anything** - open any of them and a row of folder tabs appears at the top left: Settings, Portfolio, Targets, Calls, Futures, News, Base rates. One press moves you straight across. Before this, going from the news to the portfolio meant closing one and remembering the key for the other
- **On a phone the corner controls are one button** - nine small icons used to fill the entire top edge of a phone screen. They are one button now, and it opens a list that names each panel in words rather than leaving you to recognise an icon. On a wider screen the buttons stay as they were, because seven of them fit there and one press beats two
- **You can drag the tabs into the order you want** - and the corner controls and the phone's menu follow it, because it is one order rather than three. Settings stays first
- **The chart's own settings now open beside the chart instead of over it** - press **V**, or the sliders control in the corner beside the gear, and a drawer slides in from the right with the nine settings that change the chart: candlesticks, colour, volume, grid, log scale, the average line, the details under the price, the move marks and the quiet controls. Nothing dims behind it, so you watch the chart redraw as you flip a switch - which is the one thing a settings panel covering the chart could never do. They are the same nine controls as Settings, not a copy of them
- **The panel behind "B" now names the shape today's candle makes, and counts what followed it** - fifteen of the patterns people are taught to trade, read off the daily candle: engulfing, momentum, doji and its variants, hammer, shooting star, tweezers, runs of long wicks, marubozu. Each row says what the shape is *said* to mean, how many times this coin has made it, how often the price was higher three days later, and what an ordinary three days looks like for comparison. Most of the time the answer is that it made no difference, and where a shape has happened too rarely to say anything the comparison is left out rather than dressed up. Over ten years of Bitcoin, the price rose *less* often after a "bullish" engulfing candle than on an ordinary day, and more often after a "bearish" one
- **The candle chart says where the last candle closed** - a line across the plot at that price and a tag at the right edge carrying the number, green or red by the way that candle went. The chart had no level on it at all before: the price at the top of the screen never said *where* on the plot it was. The bars keep clear of the tag rather than being drawn under it
- **Market structure, under the crowd reading on the derivatives page** - three lines from Bybit's own daily history: where today's funding sits against its last 60 days, where open interest sits, and the pair (funding high or low with open interest rising or falling over the last day). Each says how many times the market was there before, how often the price was higher three days later against an ordinary day, and how often it had fallen past 3% by then, always with the counts. It leans only on its record, like the crowd reading, and most of the time it says so
- **The news panel is a reading room** - from a wide window it takes the screen: the feed on the left, and beside it what the feed adds up to - how many of the stories on screen are worded up, worded down or neither; which of your coins the stories name, with a count each (press one to narrow the list); every source that was asked, with when it last spoke; and the permission line. A third row of pills narrows the list by wording
- **Every headline carries a tone mark, counted from its words** - *worded up* for surges, approvals, inflows and all-time highs, *worded down* for hacks, lawsuits, delistings and liquidations, with a word flipped by a “not” before it, and the words it counted in the tooltip. No AI service and no request: it is done on your device, and it says how a story is worded, never what happened or what a price will do
- **A sixth source that needs no permission: CryptoPotato** - a crypto newsroom read through the same public endpoint its site uses, with its press-release category never fetched
- **Settings → Permissions** - every permission PriceTab can hold on one tab, each with why it is asked for, what leaves your device, an Active / Off state and the buttons that grant it and take it back. The six newsrooms are one switch each. The tab also says what needs no permission at all, and why there is no proxy: every request leaves your own browser with your own address, so a limit is yours alone
- **The news panel asks once** - the card offering the six newsrooms has *Not now*; after it the card is gone for good and one line points at Settings → Permissions
- **A host that says slow down is left alone** - a feed or a widget's data source that answers 429 or 403 is not asked again for fifteen minutes, doubling to two hours; the source's chip and the widget card say *paused* with the wait, and Settings → Permissions lists what is paused right now. The news poll is also spread a little either side of ten minutes
- **A target can carry a note, a span and a repeat** - the form has a second line: a one-line note to yourself ("breakout retest"), printed on the row where it can be read back; how long to keep the target - until it is hit, a day, a week or 30 days - after which it moves to an Expired section with a way to re-arm it instead of sitting armed forever; and, for a price target, *repeat*: it re-arms itself once the price is back on the other side of the line and reports every crossing, with the row counting how many times it has spoken. These are what TradingView calls an alert's message, expiration and "every time", and what CoinGecko calls a recurring alert
- **How sure you were, and whether you were right to be** - an open call has three chips, hunch / likely / sure. Settled calls are then broken down under the scoreboard *by confidence* - the calibration table forecasting sites keep: of the calls you marked sure, how many came true - and *by coin* and *by range*, each as a count with what it is a count of, and a percentage only from five calls. The record can also be exported as a file; it stays yours and is worth nothing
- **PriceTab for iPhone** - the same app, packaged for iOS in `ios/`: the page runs inside the app with its own persistent storage (settings, coins, targets, calls, the portfolio and the practice account all survive relaunches), and a home-screen widget shows your coins' prices in the app's own order and currency, refreshed from Coinbase every fifteen minutes, with a long-press option to pin one coin. Built from the extension's own `src/` by a script; nothing in the extension changed. `ios/README.md` walks through installing it on your own phone with Xcode
- **The assistant draws what it is talking about** - hover a row that is about a price and the market chart shows the level it names and the band of ordinary steps around it - the stop sitting inside the noise, the liquidation against the window's steps, the value area the location is read against; press On the chart to keep it there. The chips under the market strip do the same on hover
- **The assistant shows on the chart** - what is missing and what has a clock on it appear as chips under the market strip, on the chart card where the eye already is, and each opens the Assistant tab; the outlook's next horizon is drawn as a band at the chart's right edge, 5th to 95th with the middle half darker
- **An assistant on the derivatives page - it reports, it never advises** - a new Assistant tab runs the checks a desk runs, as facts with their counts, in the order a desk runs them. Before a contract: whether the ticket has a stop and where it sits against the window's ordinary steps ("3 of 299 steps were at least that big"), the loss at the stop against the plan's share, the margin against the free balance, the liquidation against the window, the next funding settlement with what it costs this contract, the crowd when it is at an extreme, the regime, the location, the record's losing streak, contracts all facing one move, what was written in the last six hours, and the clock beside the bar's volume percentile. While a contract is open: no stop, no exit, liquidation against the window, the margin band, how much of its best it has given back, R now, how long it has run and what funding is due. After: the grades and your own record by setup. What is missing is missing, what has a clock on it is a watch, the rest is context - nothing says which way, every threshold is printed, and the one press a missing stop offers lands in the stop field
- **An outlook before you open anything** - a new Outlook tab on the derivatives page replays the range on the chart forward two thousand times from the last price, in blocks of its own steps, and shows where those replays landed at the next hour, four hours, day or week: a band from the 5th to the 95th, the median, and how many landed higher. When enough bars sat where the price sits now it replays only those, and says so. Under it, the regime the range is in (the last 24 bars against the 96 before, with the percentile and the follow-through), where the price sits against the value area of the bars before it, where that state went next as counted transitions, and what followed the last times the market entered it. Every figure carries its count and its window, none of them says which way, and the closing line says what none of it is
- **The ticket reads the setup, and stamps it on the contract** - four rows above the order button, the questions a process trader asks before pressing: the environment, the location against value, what the tape and the book are doing, whether a stop is on the ticket and its loss within the plan. Each is a fact with a short code, the codes are stamped on the contract and travel to the record, and the ticket prints your own record with those codes present - how your contracts did when the tape was buying, when you had no stop - once there is one. Your own base rate, by setup
- **An Outlook widget on the new tab** - the chart's range replayed forward: the 5th to 95th band at the next horizon as the figure, the median and the share above per horizon, and the count it came from. Reads the chart already drawn, so it costs nothing; Settings → Widgets → Market
- **The record grades the process, not the money** - every closed contract on the derivatives page now carries a letter: A had a stop, kept it and risked within the plan's share; B risked more than that share or sized up straight after a loss; C had no stop, moved it away from the price, or was the third loss in a row. The letter's reasons are in its tooltip. Above the list the grades are counted, and the losses that were a C are counted apart as bad losses - the ones the process took, not the market. From five closed contracts on it also prints the average R (result over what the first stop risked), the profit factor, and the win rate with the range five or fifty contracts can honestly claim; under five it says what it is waiting for instead. And Account gains one optional rule that no venue has, off unless you choose it: pause after two or three losses in a row, lifted by the next contract that does not lose
- **Three readings from the market itself, on the derivatives page** - the chart now marks where the range did its business: the price with the most volume and the band that held 70% of it. Under the order book, the Trades tab sums the last prints into a delta and a buyers' share. And an open contract carries an outcome cone: the next hour replayed two thousand times from the range on screen, through the same rules the account runs on - how many replays touched the liquidation first, the stop, the take, and where the middle of the rest ended. Every one of them is a count with its window named; none of them says which way
- **The portfolio's small chart reads on hover** - the date, the value and the move since the range began, without opening the full chart
- **The derivatives page works like a trading screen** - every open contract sits in a table under the chart, with one button to close them all (pressed twice, so it is never an accident); a line above the chart gives the last and mark price, the day's change and the next funding payment counting down. A stop, a take-profit or a waiting limit order can be dragged up and down the chart, and a move that would close the contract on the spot is refused with the reason. The ticket can size an order by what you are willing to lose at the stop, with both fees counted; it can wait for a break ("long if it breaks 105,000"), and it can be reduce-only, so a waiting take-profit can never open a contract the other way. An open contract can be reversed in two presses. Beside the order book is a tab of the market's latest trades. B and S pick the side, 1 to 4 the size, Enter the order button and X the open contract. And a closed contract can carry a note and up to three tags, with the record filterable by tag, so you can write down why while it is still fresh
- **A move target can watch an hour or four, not only a day** - “BTC falls 5%” always meant “in 24 hours”, which is one of three questions people actually ask. The form now has the window beside the direction — **in 1h**, **in 4h** or **in 24h** — and every target says which one it is watching, so two 5% targets are never confused for each other. Targets you already have keep measuring over a day, because that is what they were set to watch. A shorter window is found in the hourly candles rather than on the live price, so it is reported within the hour, and the target says so instead of quoting a 24-hour figure it is not measuring
- **The derivatives screen says what is left, and what it is left of** - the free balance is the figure that decides whether an order can be placed, and it is what the 25/50/75/100% size chips take their share of - but it was printed in the same quiet grey as the note beside it, and on its own. `$6,057.28 free` tells you nothing unless you already know whether the account is $6,100 or $60,000, so both places that print it now read **$6,057.28 free of $9,996.06** and print it in full ink at the size the number deserves. The total is your money as it stands - what is free plus what is behind your open contracts - and not the equity, which moves with every tick while you are still sizing the order
- **An average line, and it tells you how long it averages** - the trend under a day's noise is hard to see by eye, so Settings → The chart has an **Average Line**: a dashed line through the middle of what the price has been doing. What it is deliberately not is a “50-day” or “200-day” line. Those names mean days, and a point on this chart is half a minute on the hour range and a fortnight on ALL - the same “200” would cover three hours on one range and eight years on the next while looking like one indicator. This one averages a sixth of whatever you are looking at and prints what that came to on the line itself: *avg 10 min* on the hour, *avg 2 months* on the year. So it means the same thing on every range, and you never have to guess what it is measuring. It steps aside while you are comparing two coins, where the axis is percent change and an average of one of them would be a line about nothing
- **A logarithmic price axis, for the ranges that need one — press Y** - on the ALL range an ordinary axis spends almost all of its height on the most recent prices: measured on Bitcoin's own history, the first half of it takes 16% of the chart. On a log axis the same half takes 72%, and the early years become something you can actually read. The two answer different questions — an ordinary axis shows what the money did, this one shows what the rate did — and under a year they look the same, so it is off unless you turn it on, in Settings → The chart or with **Y**. It switches itself off while the calls board or the grid is up, because those draw squares of a fixed price step and this axis has no fixed step
- **Two more Bitcoin widgets: the mempool and the difficulty** - the fee card tells you what a transaction costs and never why. **BTC Mempool** is the why: how deep the queue is in blocks, how many transactions are waiting, and what they are paying — a full mempool is a fee about to rise and an empty one is a fee about to fall, whatever the current rate says. **BTC Difficulty** is the other clock Bitcoin runs on: how much harder mining is about to get, when the change lands, and what the last one was. Both come from a source PriceTab already uses, so they cost no new permission and nothing extra at rest
- **Thirty-two more coins can be tracked in the portfolio** - TRON, Hyperliquid, Bittensor, Kaspa, Ethereum Classic, Render, Dash, Flare, Decred, Raydium and more. They are priced from data PriceTab already downloads, so they cost nothing extra and need no new permission. What they do not have is a chart: no exchange PriceTab reads publishes a price history for them, so they are counted in your holdings, your total and your allocation, and left out of the value chart. The search says **no chart** on those rows before you add one, rather than leaving you to find out afterwards
- **Everything PriceTab knows can be saved to a file, and read back** - until now only the portfolio could be exported, so a new machine or a cleared profile took your price targets, your calls record, your practice account, your widget layout and every preference with it. Settings → Preferences → **Your data** now writes all of it to one file, and reads one back on another machine. Restoring takes two presses: PriceTab reads the file and tells you how many settings are in it and when it was made before anything is replaced, and what it replaced can be undone for a day afterwards. Money in now starts a new session instead: the loss that ended the last one is set aside so the limit counts afresh, and **nothing else is touched** - the record, the balance and every recorded contract survive. It looked exactly like "a position is already open and a new one will not open". The panel now decides where to land from the coin you are actually looking at: it has a position, you get the list; it does not, you get the form. Holding BTC is no longer a reason to hide the ETH form

## [1.5.0] - Predicting less, saying more (packaged 26 Aug 2026, not yet submitted)

### Added
- **"Return p.a." — the figure your bank prints and your crypto app does not** - every percentage in a portfolio tracker divides what it is worth by what you paid, which quietly treats money you put in this morning the same as money you put in two years ago. This one counts *when* the money went in. Two positions that both read "+20%" can be a 9.6%-a-year holding and a 17.8%-a-year one, and the difference is entirely the timing you chose. It is annualised, so a three-month position and a three-year one can finally be compared, and it is not shown at all until a position is two weeks old, because annualising three days of anything is arithmetic rather than information. Purchases you never dated cannot be placed on a timeline; the tooltip says how many were left out rather than quietly dropping them
- **A profit-and-loss view of your portfolio** - a third button beside Total and By coin. It is the same total with what you paid subtracted, so the line sits above or below zero and you can see at a glance whether you are up. The value chart could not tell you this: it draws what you paid as a horizontal line, and a line off the top of the scale is not drawn at all, so a portfolio deep under water could look like a perfectly healthy green wave. Zero is always on this scale, which costs the zoom - the same trade the stacked view makes, and for the same reason
- **The portfolio chart's price labels no longer sit on top of the chart** - they were drawn two pixels inside the plot, so the longer ones ran off the left edge and were cut, and what survived was printed over the data. They have their own column now
- **A band showing how far this coin usually travels, on the calls board** - turn it on in the calls panel under Show. It shades the middle half and the middle 80% of the distances this coin has actually covered over each square's worth of time, opening out of the current price. It is deliberately **not** a prediction and cannot become one: the direction is removed from the figures before they are drawn, so a coin that has risen all week produces a band that leans neither way. What it answers is the question the board could never answer by itself - whether a square four hours out is a near-certainty or a long shot, which is a completely different thing on Dogecoin and on a stablecoin. It costs nothing to have on, it is worked out from the prices already on your screen, and where there is too little history to measure a horizon the band simply stops rather than guessing
- **Every headline now carries a summary, and says which of your coins it is about** - the news panel showed the source, the age and the headline; it now shows the two-line summary the feed was already sending and was being thrown away, plus a small chip for each of the coins you follow that the story mentions. Hovering a row gives the exact date and time rather than only "5h"

It now reads the category an outlet files a story under, and the disclosure a guest post opens with. Four tokens added earlier also got their full names, so they can be found by typing the name instead of only the ticker
- **The worst fall, as a widget** - the deepest drop from a high to a later low inside the range you are looking at, for the coin on screen. It costs nothing to have on: it is worked out from the prices already drawn. This is the risk figure rather than an entry point, and that is a finding rather than a preference - when trading rules were tested properly the one thing they reliably did was cut the worst fall, while only half of them beat simply holding. A range that only went up says so instead of printing zero
- **What it costs to move your coins, on the widget panel** - two new cards: Ethereum gas and Bitcoin fees. Each shows the price the network is quoting and, underneath, roughly what that means for an ordinary transfer in your currency and how soon it would go through - because nobody prices a transfer from a gwei figure in their head. Neither asks for a permission or a key: both come from places PriceTab was already reading. It's now built from the line itself at every frame, so the two cannot disagree, and the change costs less work per frame than the two of them arguing did

Targets are now checked while the tab is hidden as well. This stays a zero-permission extension — writing your own tab's title needs no permission, which is the whole reason the feature is in-tab rather than a browser notification
- **…and you can switch it off** - Settings → Preferences → "Announce Targets In The Tab Title". - **Fixed: selling reported a gain on coins you no longer had** - there was no way to record a sale, so selling meant editing the amount down by hand — and that left the purchase lots untouched. A holding bought at 30,000 and sold in half still carried the full original cost basis, so a position now worth 45,000 kept reporting the whole position's 60,000 gain. Cost basis is now clamped to what you actually hold, wherever the mismatch came from: a recorded sale, a hand edit, or an import
- **Record a sale, and see what you actually made** - the expanded holding takes "Sold" as well as "Bought" — the same two questions, how much and for how much. Recording one does the three things that have to happen together: takes the coins off, consumes the oldest purchases against them (FIFO), and keeps the resulting gain, which the lots it consumed can no longer be asked for. A **Realized** figure joins the portfolio stats: settled money, sitting beside the unrealized position rather than folded into it. Selling coins you never logged a purchase for is handled honestly — the gain covers the part that had a cost, and the rest is reported as proceeds with nothing to set against them
- **"Tax report" is now "Cost basis report"** - the old name promised a document you could file from, and the file is the record one is worked out *from*: it holds only what you entered in PriceTab, with no exchange history, transfers, fees, crypto-to-crypto trades or staking income. The caveat also moved to where it gets read — on the button, before the click, rather than in a footnote inside a file you've already downloaded — and the file now opens by saying what it is and what it doesn't contain
- **The cost basis report pairs each sale with the purchases it consumed** - a disposal on its own is half a record. Every tax form and every tax tool asks the same thing in the same shape: which acquisition, which disposal, what it cost, what it fetched. A single line per sale can't answer that, because one sale can eat several purchases bought on different days at different prices — and each of those pairs has its own holding period, so the same sale can be part long-term and part short. The Disposals table is now one line per pair, with the acquisition date, the sale date, the days held and the term. Proceeds are split across the pairs by amount, which is arithmetic rather than an allocation: a sale happens at one price, so every unit fetched the same. Lines that have no acquisition to name are still emitted — the uncovered part of a sale, and sales recorded before the pairing existed — because a proceeds column that doesn't add up to what you received is the first thing anyone checks
- **The report has a Disposals section** - it used to state outright that it recorded no sales, which made it half a report. Every recorded sale is now a line with its date, proceeds, the basis it consumed and the realized gain, and the summary block carries the total. The disclaimer that replaced it is the accurate one: sales are in there if you recorded them, and anything sold outside PriceTab is not
- **The portfolio chart says something now** - the full-bleed chart behind the portfolio was texture: no axis, no readout, nothing you could take a number off. It draws only while the level is inside the range on screen — a line pinned to the edge would claim a crossing the window doesn't contain
- **The portfolio stats found a shape** - seven equal-weight figures in one flat run had grown to about 950px in a 760px column, wrapping into two ragged lines of 10px uppercase with nothing to say where to look first. - **Holdings are ordered by what they're worth** - the list rendered in the order coins were added, so your biggest position could sit at the bottom — while the chart behind it was already ranking the same holdings by value to decide which twelve to draw. Biggest first is now the default, with one-tap ordering by value, P/L, today's move or name, remembered between visits. It can't be read for the part that was actually yours: whether holding *these* coins beat holding the obvious one. The stats row now carries the gap in percentage points over whichever range the chart is on, with both numbers spelled out on hover. The comparison is exact rather than indicative — the value series holds amounts fixed across the window, so there are no deposits or withdrawals inside it to distort either side, and the benchmark is trimmed to the portfolio's own window so it can't win on span. The previous order goes into the same undo the reset uses, so a sort can't silently discard an arrangement you dragged into place
- **The report knows how long you've held things** - short-term versus long-term is the split a return actually turns on, and the CSV had no idea: it listed each purchase's date and left the arithmetic to you. Every lot now carries its days held and which side of the one-year mark it falls on, plus its own current value and unrealized gain, so the sheet can be sorted or filtered on the split without recomputing anything.
